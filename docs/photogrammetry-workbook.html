Chapter 00 · Orientation

The whole journey

By the end, explain why Echo needs several different representations of the same cliff.

Explain it to a ten-year-old

You and a friend photograph a climbing wall from different places. You recognise the same holds in both photos. Those shared clues help you work out where you were standing and where the holds are. You add more detail, build a triangle skin over it, and put the photographs’ colours onto that skin.

A photograph flattens a 3D scene onto a 2D image. Photogrammetry tries to recover the missing spatial information using agreement between overlapping views. The challenge is circular: camera locations help locate scene points, and scene points help locate cameras. The first part of the pipeline solves this relationship using a manageable set of distinctive landmarks. Later stages estimate far more surface detail.

  1. 1 → FeaturesPhotographs → recognisable 2D details
  2. 2 → MatchingDetails → links between photographs
  3. 3 → AlignmentLinks → cameras + sparse 3D points
  4. 4 → DepthAligned photos → per-view distances
  5. 5 → Dense cloudDistances → validated coloured dots
  6. 6 → FusionDepth evidence → a shared 3D field
  7. 7 → MeshField → connected triangle surface
  8. 8 → TextureSurface + photos → photographic skin
  9. 9 → ExportGeometry + colour → portable model

Three ideas that organise everything

Correspondence: which observations refer to the same physical thing? Geometry: where are the cameras and surfaces? Appearance: what colours should a viewer display? These questions interact, but success at one does not guarantee success at the others. A realistic photograph projected onto a flat plane can look convincing from one angle.

How to use this workbook

On your first pass, read the green concept boxes and inspect the diagrams. On a second pass, follow the numbered algorithms and work the examples. On a third pass, open a saved Echo reconstruction and answer each chapter’s inspection question. Write your answer before opening the suggested answer; being able to explain the process is more useful than recognising the terminology.

The sidebar checkboxes track review, not mastery. Each chapter includes space for an explanation, a sketch description or observations from your own project. Export your notes as JSON for a portable backup. Import replaces notes for matching workbook fields and asks before doing so.

Review 00

A model has beautiful photographic cracks but looks flat when shown without its texture. Which representation was hiding the problem?

Suggested answer

The texture supplied the visual cracks while the mesh lacked their geometric relief. Inspect depth maps, the dense cloud and the untextured mesh to find where shape detail was lost. Increasing texture resolution cannot create missing relief.

Chapter 01 · Before the pipeline

Cameras, capture & parallax

Distinguish image position, camera pose, focal length, depth and real-world scale.

Explain it to a ten-year-old

A photo tells you which direction a rock is in, but not how far away it is. A second photo from another place gives another direction. Together, the directions can locate the rock.

The pinhole-camera idea

Imagine a tiny hole in a box. Light from a scene point passes through the hole to a spot on the image. A simplified camera model uses this geometry even though a real camera has a lens. In camera coordinates, a point is (X, Y, Z); Z is its forward depth. Its image position depends on the ratios X/Z and Y/Z.

u = f × X / Z + cₓ
v = f × Y / Z + cᵧ

Here u, v are pixel coordinates, f is focal length measured in pixels, and cₓ, cᵧ locate the image centre. Focal length in pixels is different from a lens’s millimetre specification: it relates viewing angle to image resolution. These internal camera properties are intrinsics. The camera’s position and rotation in the scene are extrinsics, or its pose.

Echo’s model

Echo’s projection uses a shared focal value for the horizontal and vertical directions and assumes the principal point is at the image centre. Alignment can refine focal lengths. The reconstructor explicitly reports that it does not perform lens-distortion calibration or survey control. This is a practical limitation to remember with strongly distorted lenses or measurement-sensitive work.

Parallax and baseline

Baseline is the distance between camera positions. Parallax is the change in a point’s viewing direction. A distant cliff seen from nearly identical locations produces weak parallax. Small errors in feature location can then create large errors in estimated depth. A wider baseline can improve geometric leverage, but excessive viewpoint change makes appearance matching harder and reveals different occlusions.

Explore · Simplified parallel cameras

This teaching model fixes focal length at 1,000 pixels. Change baseline or depth to see how disparity changes. Echo handles general camera poses; this schematic is not to scale and isolates one relationship.

Disparity = 100 px · about 0.10 m depth change for 1 px disparity error

For parallel, rectified cameras: disparity d = f × B / Z
Depth Z = f × B / d
Small-error estimate: |ΔZ| ≈ Z² × |Δd| / (f × B)

These formulas explain why distance hurts depth precision and why resizing images affects the pixel-scale calculation. They assume a rectified stereo arrangement; do not insert arbitrary raw-image coordinate differences into them.

Capture as a connected network

Photographs should form a chain of overlapping observations with redundant connections. A single bridge photo between two otherwise separate groups is fragile: if that photo fails to match, the reconstruction can split. Move between exposures, retain substantial overlap, and include different heights or angles when you need overhangs. Photographing only the front cannot measure a hidden back surface.

Stable, sharp, textured surfaces are easier than shiny, transparent or moving ones. Repeated patterns can create false matches. A blank wall offers little evidence. These are failures of available information, not merely settings that need increasing.

Worked example · Scale ambiguity

A toy cliff photographed nearby and a much larger cliff photographed farther away can produce equivalent image geometry. Visual reconstruction therefore needs an external length or position reference to establish physical scale. Echo can use GPS/hints, but approximate location information does not make the resulting model survey-grade.

  1. With f = 1,000 px, B = 1 m and d = 50 px, what is depth?
  2. What happens to disparity if baseline doubles?
  3. Why does rotating a camera in one place fail to provide a useful translational baseline?
Suggested answers

1. Depth is 20 m. 2. It doubles to 100 px for the same point in this simplified geometry. 3. The camera centre does not move, so the new image does not add a second spatial origin for triangulation. Rotation can reveal more scene coverage but does not by itself determine depths.

Chapter 02 · Pipeline stage: features

Features & descriptors

Explain the difference between finding an interesting location and describing its appearance.

Explain it to a ten-year-old

First, circle recognisable spots in each photograph. Then write a short description of the pattern around each spot so you can find it in another photograph—even if it is bigger or turned around.

Detection versus description

A detector proposes locations. A descriptor turns the nearby appearance into numbers. A point’s image coordinates identify where it is within one image; its descriptor helps establish which point it corresponds to in another. A descriptor is not a unique physical ID: different rock cracks can have similar patterns.

A long, uniform edge is hard to locate along its own direction. A corner, junction or distinct blob supplies more localisation information. Good features should recur across images and remain recognisable under moderate viewpoint, scale and lighting changes. No detector is invariant to every change.

Echo’s scale-space algorithm

  1. Prepare the image. Resize and convert to greyscale. Matching initially depends on intensity structure.
  2. Build a Gaussian pyramid. Make progressively blurred versions. A Gaussian blur averages nearby pixels with greater weight near the centre.
  3. Subtract neighbouring blur levels. These difference-of-Gaussian images emphasise structures at particular sizes.
  4. Find scale-space extrema. Look for distinctive maxima or minima across image position and blur level. Downsample to another octave to detect larger structures economically.
  5. Estimate orientation. Build a histogram of local gradient directions and use strong peaks as reference directions.
  6. Describe the neighbourhood. Rotate the sampling frame to the reference direction, collect gradient histograms, normalise, clip large entries and normalise again.
  7. Retain the strongest candidates. Apply the configured feature budget and map coordinates back to the original image size.
Implementation detail

This is a SIFT-style implementation within Echo. The descriptor has a 4 × 4 spatial grid with 8 orientation bins per cell: 4 × 4 × 8 = 128 numbers. Echo’s orientation histogram uses 36 bins and can assign multiple strong orientations. The descriptor is L2-normalised, entries are capped at 0.2, and it is normalised again. These statements describe Echo’s code; they do not imply it uses OpenCV’s SIFT implementation.

Echo’s additional clues

Line detection is enabled by default. Echo also detects junctions where lines meet, adding potentially useful localisation cues for cracks and edges. Line descriptors are matched separately; a matched line segment should not be mistaken for a finished 3D line reconstruction. When scale-invariant features are disabled, the feature extractor takes a separate corner-based path.

Reason through it

Two photos show the same hold at 30 pixels and 60 pixels across. A fixed-size descriptor window may cover the whole hold in one photo and only its centre in the other. Detecting an appropriate scale and sampling a corresponding neighbourhood makes the descriptions more comparable.

Inspect in Echo: are features spread across the rock you care about, or mostly on trees and background detail? A high count concentrated in one corner is weaker coverage than a well-distributed set.

Review 02

Explain why 6,000 detected features do not mean 6,000 correct matches. What would happen if you raised the feature budget on a completely blank wall?

Suggested answer

Features are independently detected candidates. They can be absent from other views, look ambiguous, belong to moving objects or be poorly localised. Increasing a budget cannot add image structure that was never captured; it may retain weaker, noisier candidates instead.

Chapter 03 · Pipeline stage: matching

Matching & geometric verification

Explain why “looks similar” and “fits the same scene geometry” are separate tests.

Explain it to a ten-year-old

Lots of jigsaw pieces have similar colours. First choose pieces that look promising. Then check whether their shapes actually fit together.

First choose which photographs to compare

For N photos, comparing every unordered pair gives N(N−1)/2 pairs. That is 4,950 pairs for 100 photos and 499,500 for 1,000. Most may not overlap. Echo selects candidates using capture sequence, spatial proximity, viewing overlap and supplied hints, with an all-pairs setting available. Pair selection can omit a useful connection, so matching quality depends on this earlier decision too.

Three descriptor checks

  1. Nearest neighbour: find the descriptor with the smallest difference.
  2. Mutual agreement: A’s best candidate must also choose A as its best candidate. This discourages many details collapsing onto one lookalike.
  3. Ambiguity and distance: the best candidate must beat the runner-up sufficiently and be close enough in absolute descriptor distance.
Echo descriptor distance D(a,b) = Σᵢ(aᵢ − bᵢ)²
Ratio condition: Dbest < threshold × Dsecond
Absolute condition: Dbest ≤ distance limit

Echo uses squared Euclidean distances here. Its default ratio threshold of 0.72 is applied directly to those squared values; it corresponds to about 0.849 on ordinary Euclidean-distance ratios. Do not copy a threshold from another implementation without checking which distance it uses.

Explore · Ambiguous or distinctive?

The best squared distance is fixed at 0.20. Move the runner-up’s squared distance. The other descriptor gates are assumed to pass; geometric verification still comes later.

0.20 / 0.50 = 0.40 → passes the 0.72 ratio test

Epipolar geometry: a powerful constraint

A point in photo A defines a ray into the world. From camera B, possible positions along that ray project onto an epipolar line. Its matching observation should lie on that line. A fundamental matrix, F, encodes this relation between two images without needing the final 3D scene.

x′ᵀ F x ≈ 0, where x = [u, v, 1]ᵀ

The exact equation is less important than its role: a match must fit a common relationship shared by the pair, not merely resemble one patch. Noise means matches lie near the line rather than exactly on it. Echo uses a Sampson-style error, an approximation to geometric correction error, to score agreement.

RANSAC: find a trustworthy consensus

  1. Randomly select a small subset of candidate matches.
  2. Fit a model to that subset.
  3. Count how many other matches agree within a tolerance: the inliers.
  4. Repeat, keeping a strong consensus and refining it using its inliers.
Echo’s specific path

Echo fits the fundamental matrix with normalised eight-point samples, requires at least 12 supporting matches for an estimate, and adapts its RANSAC iteration limit. Promising consensuses are refitted. The pixel threshold scales with image size. Matching records verification flags; alignment rechecks geometry and consumes the filtered matches. Manual tie points are retained as explicit constraints but do not count as proof of an automatically verified geometric fit.

Why normalisation matters: raw pixel coordinates can be large and unevenly distributed. Moving and scaling them into a better numerical range makes the matrix estimation better conditioned. Why RANSAC is not a guarantee: repeated structure or poorly conditioned geometry can produce a misleading consensus. The later reconstruction still needs triangulation and consistency checks.

Review 03

Candidate A has best/second squared distances 0.20/0.25. Candidate B has 0.20/0.50. Which passes Echo’s 0.72 ratio check? Why can the passing match still be wrong?

Suggested answer

A has ratio 0.80 and fails. B has ratio 0.40 and passes. B is distinctive among the candidates, but its best candidate can still be a wrong lookalike, a moving object or a false geometric correspondence. Descriptor tests are necessary filters, not physical proof.

Chapter 04 · Pipeline stage: alignment

Locating cameras, refining structure

Understand triangulation, camera registration, reprojection error and bundle adjustment as parts of one estimation problem.

Explain it to a ten-year-old

You have photographs taken from unknown places. Move pretend cameras and pretend landmarks around until the pretend photos line up with the real ones. First get a rough arrangement; then carefully improve it.

4.1 Establish a starting arrangement

Optimisation needs a useful starting point. Echo can seed camera poses from available location and attitude information. Otherwise it tries strongly matched image pairs. With camera intrinsics, their fundamental matrix can be converted into an essential matrix, which constrains relative rotation and translation direction. Recovering a relative pose does not supply an absolute distance between cameras.

Echo decomposes the relative geometry into candidate poses and tests points in front of the cameras. This is the cheirality check: a mathematically possible solution with the scene behind a camera is not a physically useful reconstruction.

4.2 Triangulate sparse landmarks

Cast viewing rays from the cameras through corresponding image locations. Real rays usually do not meet perfectly because of noise, lens-model error and imperfect camera estimates. Triangulation chooses a point consistent with those rays and checks its projections. A tiny intersection angle is unstable: a small angular change can move the intersection a long way.

4.3 Register additional cameras

Once some 3D points exist, find their corresponding 2D observations in a new photo. Estimating a camera from these 3D-to-2D correspondences is the camera-resection or Perspective-n-Point problem. Echo’s registration path uses calibrated direct linear estimation with RANSAC, followed by the shared refinement process. It is not necessary to claim a particular library PnP solver to understand the role.

4.4 Bundle adjustment

Reprojection means projecting an estimated 3D point back into an estimated camera. Reprojection error is the distance in pixels from that projection to the actual observed feature. Bundle adjustment changes camera and point parameters together to reduce these discrepancies across the whole set of observations.

Conceptual objective:
sum of robust reprojection penalties + camera/focal prior penalties
Worked example · A single observation

A detected feature is at pixel (400, 300). The current model projects the landmark to (403, 304). The reprojection error is √(3² + 4²) = 5 pixels. Moving the landmark can reduce that error in one photo but increase it in another. Joint optimisation finds a compromise across the observations.

Echo groups observations into tracks, representing one landmark across multiple photos. It adjusts camera positions and rotations, landmark positions, and shared focal parameters for matching camera groups. GPS acts as a soft prior where available: the optimisation can trade a position change against image agreement. It also restrains focal changes.

Inside the solver: damping, robust weights and the Schur complement

Echo builds a local quadratic approximation to the current fitting problem. Large image residuals receive reduced influence through robust weighting. Damping, controlled by a parameter often called lambda, makes proposed updates more conservative when a solve is unreliable or a step worsens the objective.

The point variables occur in small blocks. Echo eliminates those blocks to form a camera system, solves that system with Cholesky factorisation, then recovers point updates. This block elimination is the Schur-complement idea: exploit the problem’s structure rather than solve one undifferentiated giant system.

Improving steps are accepted and damping is reduced; failed steps are rejected and damping increases. This is a Levenberg–Marquardt-style strategy. A good initialisation still matters because a local optimiser cannot guarantee the globally correct scene.

4.5 Guided recovery and a second assessment

Echo’s optional guided pass uses estimated pair geometry to constrain another search for matches. Recovered correspondences are checked against established 3D landmarks before another joint refinement. An evaluator compares the result with the established alignment; a rejected or unstable recovery leaves the original verified scene in place. The setting is enabled by default in this revision.

Inspect in Echo: camera placement should form a plausible capture path, and the sparse cloud should resemble the intended scene. Check unplaced images and the bundle report. Low reprojection error alone does not prove correct scale, complete coverage or absence of systematic lens error.

Chapter 05 · Pipeline stage: depth

Depth from multiple views

Explain how a depth hypothesis becomes a patch comparison, and why missing depth can be a sensible result.

Explain it to a ten-year-old

Pretend a patch of rock is 5 metres away, then 6, then 7. For each guess, work out where it would appear in the other photographs. Keep a distance only when the pictures agree well enough.

5.1 Plan the search

Echo uses the aligned sparse scene to estimate a useful distance interval, unless configured to use a manual one. It selects neighbouring cameras with compatible viewing directions and favours useful separation when suitable candidates exist. The closest camera is not always the best stereo partner: its baseline may be too small to constrain depth.

5.2 Search in inverse depth

The global candidate depths are spaced uniformly in inverse depth, 1/Z. In simple stereo geometry, disparity is proportional to inverse depth, so this spreads candidates more evenly in image displacement than uniform metre steps. This is a depth-hypothesis search using fronto-parallel patches, not a claim that the code runs a generic PatchMatch implementation.

Worked example · Five search planes

For an interval from 2 to 10 scene units, inverse depth runs from 0.5 to 0.1. Five equally spaced inverse-depth samples are 0.5, 0.4, 0.3, 0.2 and 0.1. Their depths are 2, 2.5, 3.33, 5 and 10. Notice how the depth gaps widen farther from the camera.

5.3 Compare a warped patch

  1. Take a 5 × 5 set of greyscale samples around the reference pixel. Subtract its average brightness.
  2. For a proposed depth, place that small patch on a plane facing the reference camera.
  3. Project its samples into a neighbouring camera using the estimated camera geometry.
  4. Sample the neighbour’s image and compare the brightness variation using normalised cross-correlation.
  5. Repeat across depths and views, retaining a supported low-cost candidate.
Patch cost = 1 − normalised correlation
Similar brightness patterns → correlation near 1 → cost near 0

Normalisation makes the comparison less sensitive to simple brightness shifts and scaling. It does not fix strong shadows, reflections, changed content or incorrect geometry. A patch with almost no intensity variation has little evidence to correlate. Echo rejects such weak patches.

Echo’s acceptance logic

When enough usable neighbours exist, the cost combines the two best supporting views, allowing another neighbour to be occluded or unsuitable. Poor matches are rejected. During global search, comparably good but geometrically distinct alternatives are treated as ambiguous. A local interpolation around a well-shaped cost minimum can refine the selected depth between sampled hypotheses.

5.4 Coarse-to-fine refinement

Echo starts at a small image width, up to 160 pixels, and increases resolution to the configured depth width. Coarse estimates guide a smaller search at the next level. If a coarse estimate is missing or rejected, the finer level can search the full interval again: failure at a blurry level does not establish that no surface exists. Unsupported samples are filtered at each level.

The resulting depth is measured along the camera’s forward axis in this model, rather than always being the Euclidean range along a normalised ray. Off-axis pixels therefore need the camera model when converted into world points.

5.5 Optional semantic filtering and cliff recovery

When enabled by relevant settings, Echo runs a local segmentation model to estimate sky, vegetation and rock labels. Sky suppression removes depth estimates in sky regions. These labels are predictions, so they can be mistaken; they do not directly supply metric depth.

Cliff recovery targets bounded missing regions classified as rock. It examines finer source imagery, searches near supported depth anchors and checks photometric and geometric support against the original depth maps. Newly recovered values do not validate one another in a circular chain. Existing valid samples are preserved. This is a targeted second measurement attempt; the later Poisson completion stage has a different role.

Confidence is not a probability. Echo’s stereo confidence is derived from matching cost. A value such as 0.92 does not mean “92% chance that the depth is correct”. Similar-looking wrong patches or incorrect camera poses can still score well.

Inspect in Echo: look for coherent surfaces, missing regions, suspicious sky depths and edges that bleed between foreground and background. Compare each suspicious region with its source photographs before changing search density.

Review 05

Two widely different depths produce almost equally good patch matches. Should the system simply choose the lower-cost one? How is cliff recovery different from smoothing a hole shut?

Suggested answer

The match is ambiguous; a tiny cost advantage may be noise. Rejecting the estimate can be safer than creating false geometry. Cliff recovery searches the photographs for additional evidence and validates it against original depth maps. Surface smoothing or completion imposes geometric continuity where measurements may still be absent.

Chapter 06 · Pipeline stage: cloud

From depth maps to coloured points

Distinguish image-space depth, world-space points and cross-view consistency.

Explain it to a ten-year-old

Each reliable distance tells you where to place a coloured pin in space. Before keeping a pin, ask whether another photograph agrees that something belongs there.

6.1 Back-project each candidate

Projection maps a 3D point into an image. Back-projection takes an image pixel and an estimated depth back into 3D. A camera-space point is constructed using the focal length and image centre, rotated into world orientation, then translated by the camera centre.

Camera point = Z × [(u−cₓ)/f, (v−cᵧ)/f, 1]
World point = camera centre + camera-to-world rotation × camera point
Worked example

At u−cₓ = 100 px, v−cᵧ = 0, f = 1,000 px, and Z = 10, the point is (1, 0, 10) in camera coordinates. Its distance from the camera centre is √101 ≈ 10.05, illustrating the difference between forward depth and radial range.

6.2 Ask neighbouring maps to corroborate it

  1. Reject invalid or non-positive depths.
  2. Construct the candidate world point.
  3. Check that a neighbouring camera provides enough angular separation to be useful.
  4. Project into that view and compare with its estimated surface depth.
  5. Reproject the neighbour’s surface estimate into the reference image and check the pixel discrepancy.
  6. Retain the point if enough neighbours support it, then attach its source colour.
Echo’s default gates

This revision considers up to 8 cloud neighbours, requires support from at least 1 neighbour, uses a relative depth tolerance of 0.01, a reprojection tolerance of 1.5 pixels and a minimum parallax angle of 0.5°. These are implementation defaults, not universal recommendations. Cloud confidence stores the fraction of selected neighbours that supported the sample; it differs from stereo matching confidence.

What “dense” does and does not mean

The cloud samples many more image locations than the sparse landmarks used for camera alignment. It still has holes where evidence fails. Multiple images can contribute nearby or overlapping samples; this cloud is not automatically a unique, perfectly uniform set of surface points. It has no triangle connectivity and does not decide what lies between points.

Inspect in Echo: a second layer behind the cliff can indicate inconsistent depths or alignment. Floating specks can indicate weak support. A large hole can result from either missing measurements or strict validation. Check earlier representations to distinguish the two.

Review 06

Why is support from a nearly identical camera position less informative than support from a view with useful parallax? If the cloud has a hole but depth maps contain samples there, where would you investigate?

Suggested answer

Nearly parallel rays constrain distance weakly and can share similar ambiguity. In the second case, investigate cloud consistency checks, neighbour selection, parallax and reprojection disagreements. The samples may exist but fail to corroborate one another.

Chapter 07 · Pipeline stage: fusion

Combining evidence in a volume

Explain a signed distance field, weighted fusion, truncation and the difference between unknown space and observed empty space.

Explain it to a ten-year-old

Several people measure the same wall, but their rulers disagree slightly. Combine the readings, trust the more precise ones more, and write the answer into a grid around the wall.

7.1 A field instead of a pile of points

A voxel is a small cell in 3D space. A signed distance field assigns distance-like values around a surface. Its zero crossing marks the surface. Echo uses a projective TSDF: truncated signed distance function. Each camera contributes distance evidence based on its depth observation. “Projective” matters: these are camera-based distance estimates, not necessarily exact shortest distances to the final surface.

7.2 Weighted integration

Fused value D = Σ(wᵢ × dᵢ) / Σwᵢ

Here each dᵢ is distance evidence at one grid location and wᵢ is its weight. The zero crossing of the combined field becomes a candidate surface. Averaging can reduce independent measurement noise, but systematic alignment errors do not disappear just because many readings are combined.

Explore · Two measurements at one location

Measurement A contributes +0.02; B contributes −0.04. Hold B’s weight at 1 and change A’s weight. Units are arbitrary scene units. This demonstrates the weighted average only, not Echo’s full integration logic.

(4 × 0.02 − 0.04) / 5 = +0.0080

7.3 Truncation and uncertainty

Distance observations are most meaningful near their measured surface. A truncation band limits their influence farther away. Echo estimates a depth uncertainty radius from both relative depth tolerance and projected pixel footprint. It derives precision from inverse squared uncertainty, then tapers weights within the integration band.

Echo’s integration choices
  • The uncertainty radius is the larger of depth × relative tolerance and approximately two projected pixels.
  • The truncation extent is the larger of three voxel widths and twice the uncertainty radius.
  • Far behind a measured surface, the observation does not vote.
  • Far in front, continuous valid coverage can contribute capped, reduced-weight free-space evidence.
  • Retained voxels need support from at least two nearby observed depths. Free-space evidence alone does not count as that near-surface support.

7.4 Resolution is a separate decision

Voxel size controls how finely the field is sampled. Measurement uncertainty controls how strongly observations are trusted and how broadly they are integrated. Smaller voxels do not make noisy measurements more accurate. Halving cell width could require roughly eight times as many cells for a fully filled fixed volume; Echo’s sparse planning and storage mean its actual growth depends on the occupied region.

Echo plans bounds and resources using the reconstruction evidence, integrates the depth maps and can remove disconnected fusion regions when configured. The dense cloud supports planning; fusion still uses depth observations and visibility, rather than simply replacing each cloud point with a cube.

The distinction to remember

Observed empty: a valid depth ray reaches the wall, so the segment before the wall provides evidence of free space. Unknown: there was no usable depth measurement. Occluded: the wall blocks what lies behind it. Treating all three as empty would erase real surfaces.

Review 07

A foreground branch hides the cliff in one camera. Should that camera vote to remove the cliff? Why can reducing voxel size increase detail and still fail to improve accuracy?

Suggested answer

No: the cliff is behind the branch’s measured surface and is occluded in that view. Smaller voxels can represent finer spatial variation, including finer noise, but do not repair incorrect depths or camera alignment. Resolution is representational capacity, while accuracy depends on the evidence and model.

Chapter 08 · Pipeline stage: mesh

Surface extraction, repair & completion

Separate extracting supported shape, reducing complexity and inferring missing surface.

Explain it to a ten-year-old

The distance grid tells you where the rock’s skin should be. Make that skin from triangles. Remove triangles that add little useful shape, fix small tears, and carefully infer some missing pieces.

8.1 Extract the zero crossing

Within a grid cell, positive and negative distance values indicate that the surface passes between them. Echo divides each eligible cube into six tetrahedra using a consistent body diagonal. For each tetrahedron, it finds edges whose endpoint signs differ and interpolates the zero position on those edges. The resulting polygon becomes one or two triangles.

Edge interpolation fraction t = valueA / (valueA − valueB)
Surface position = A + t × (B − A)
Worked example · Find the surface

An edge runs from position 0 to position 1. Its endpoint field values are +0.2 and −0.3. Then t = 0.2 / (0.2 − (−0.3)) = 0.4. The surface crosses 40% of the way along the edge. It does not have to pass through the middle.

Adjacent cells must agree on how they share faces and vertices. Echo’s consistent tetrahedral subdivision avoids contradictory face diagonals in ambiguous configurations. Shared crossings reuse vertices, including zero crossings exactly on lattice vertices. The extraction path performs bounded grid-gap repair first; absence of arbitrary distant samples is not permission to mesh all space.

8.2 Simplify with quadric error

A detailed grid can produce many more triangles than a viewer needs. Edge collapse replaces the endpoints of a mesh edge with one vertex and updates neighbouring faces. A quadric error metric summarises how far candidate vertices would move away from local surface planes. Collapsing low-error edges first usually spends fewer triangles on flat areas and retains more near changing shape.

Echo’s variation

Echo evaluates endpoint and midpoint candidates, with special handling around open boundaries. It normalises accumulated plane error and uses boundary constraints to discourage cutting away corners of otherwise flat patches. Detail tolerances and per-vertex tolerances can influence reduction. FaceLimit is a ceiling: the algorithm is not trying to manufacture exactly that many useful triangles.

This simplifier preserves an approximation of the input mesh, which may already contain mistakes. A perfectly simplified wrong surface remains wrong. Also, a texture can preserve the appearance of a tiny crack after geometric simplification removes its relief.

8.3 Repair observed holes and reject contradictions

Small holes can be supported by nearby depth evidence and repaired locally. Echo then checks surfaces against visible depth rays. If multiple views say space in front of the observed surface is empty, a triangle occupying that space is contradicted. Missing and occluded observations are treated differently from those explicit contradictions.

8.4 Screened Poisson completion

When gaps remain, Echo builds oriented samples: points with normals indicating surface direction. Poisson reconstruction fits a smooth implicit surface guided by that orientation field. The screened variant also includes point-position constraints to pull the surface toward the supplied samples. An adaptive octree concentrates solver resolution where needed.

Echo calls a bundled native Poisson worker. It chooses octree depth from scene extent and voxel scale, within bounds. This produces a reconstructed surface, not merely an operation that stitches existing boundary vertices together. Inferred regions can bridge missing coverage; their smoothness does not establish that the hidden cliff really has that shape.

A little more mathematics: what does Poisson solve?

Imagine an indicator function whose values distinguish the inside and outside of a shape. Its spatial change is concentrated near the surface. Oriented samples suggest how that function should change. Poisson reconstruction seeks a function whose gradient agrees with a vector field built from the normals, leading to a Poisson equation. Screening adds constraints at sampled points. Extracting a level set of the solution yields a surface.

You do not need to solve the equation to use the idea: normals give local orientation; a global smoothness model connects the evidence. The connection can extend into areas without measurements.

8.5 Constrain and clean the completed surface

  1. Restrict reconstruction to the neighbourhood of the observed surface. Echo’s support filter is called with a distance based on 24 voxel widths in this revision.
  2. Adaptively simplify the completed mesh.
  3. Reject completed triangles contradicted by observed empty space.
  4. Repair small enclosed holes left by cleanup and enforce the face budget as needed.
  5. Optionally simplify confirmed vegetation more aggressively while protecting relevant boundaries, and optionally trim disconnected mesh islands.

Poisson’s tendency to close a surface does not guarantee that Echo’s final mesh is watertight. Support filtering, visibility filtering and later edits can leave boundaries. Nor is a watertight mesh automatically a faithful measurement.

8.6 Select photographs for faces

Before texture baking, Echo chooses which photographs will colour the mesh. It evaluates visible candidate views and their photographic detail. A graph treats neighbouring faces as connected nodes; a label identifies the source photo for each face.

Photo-selection energy = per-face view cost + penalties for seams between neighbours

Choosing the sharpest view independently for every triangle could create a patchwork of tiny colour discontinuities. Echo’s alpha-expansion graph-cut passes consider coordinated switches of groups of faces to a photograph. Visibility remains a hard constraint, and accepted switches reduce the combined objective. This balances local image quality with coherent regions; it is not a guarantee of a globally optimal labelling.

Chapter 09 · Pipeline stage: texture

Unwrapping a photographic skin

Explain UV coordinates, chart construction, atlas packing, seam correction and texture filtering.

Explain it to a ten-year-old

Imagine cutting a paper model along a few seams and laying the pieces flat. Paint the flat pieces using the photographs, then fold them back into the model. The computer remembers which painted spot belongs on every triangle.

9.1 Geometry coordinates versus texture coordinates

XYZ describes a point in 3D. UV describes a location in a 2D image. Each mesh triangle has corresponding UV corners. A renderer interpolates those coordinates across the triangle to look up colour. A UV seam may require two texture coordinates for the same geometric position, which can duplicate vertices in the exported representation without creating a visible geometric crack.

A connected flattened patch is a chart or UV island. The image containing the packed charts is a texture atlas. A texel is one texture-image pixel. Texel density expresses how much texture resolution is allocated per unit of model surface.

9.2 Build and parameterise charts

  1. Group connected faces into regions subject to chart-size and angle constraints.
  2. Attempt a least-squares conformal map, which aims to preserve local angles while flattening.
  3. Validate the result, including unacceptable folds or distortion.
  4. If needed, try a harmonic map with a convex boundary.
  5. If the map is still unsuitable, split the region and try smaller pieces.
  6. Merge compatible charts, orient them and pass them to the packer.
Conformal and harmonic maps, without the jargon

A conformal map tries to preserve tiny local angles. Flattening a curved surface generally forces some stretching, so Echo solves an approximate least-squares problem and pins two chart vertices to remove free movement and scale. Angle preservation does not mean area preservation: some pieces can become larger or smaller.

The harmonic fallback fixes boundary vertices around a circle and places each interior vertex according to neighbouring positions. Echo uses positive-weight barycentric equations. This provides another way to avoid folds, but a non-folded map can still stretch too much; validation still matters.

9.3 Pack the atlas

Chart packing is a two-dimensional arrangement problem. It needs room for the patch shapes plus padding. Efficient packing lets more photographic detail fit into the same atlas. Too many tiny charts can spend much of the image on gutters. Echo includes shape-aware packing and chart orientation; a larger square image is not the only lever.

Optional vegetation budgeting can reduce texel allocation to confirmed vegetation. Echo compares candidate layouts, including padding costs, and keeps a candidate only when it does not reduce the baseline rock density. An apparently sensible smaller-chart arrangement is not automatically a better packing result.

9.4 Correct photographic seams

Two photos of the same surface can have different exposure, white balance or shadows. If adjacent faces use different photos, their colours may jump along the boundary. Echo samples seam relationships, solves bounded global per-image colour offsets, and adds local corrections diffused across chart interiors.

The correction is an attempt to reduce discontinuities, not to recover a physically calibrated material or undo arbitrary moving shadows. Source-photo choice, camera alignment and visibility remain important. A doubled crack due to misalignment cannot reliably be fixed by brightness adjustment.

9.5 Bake, pad and downsample

Baking writes the selected photographic information into the atlas. Sampling and antialiasing reduce jagged edges. Padding extends suitable colours into gutters so that filtered lookups near chart edges do not mix with unrelated neighbours. Echo encodes an atlas and produces progressively smaller variants with shared UV coordinates.

Some completed faces have no suitable photograph. Echo’s colour-completion path propagates and smooths boundary colours through those regions, with a fallback mean where no boundary connection supplies colour. Such colour is interpolated appearance, not recovered photographic detail. Texture sky suppression and vegetation budgeting are separate optional controls.

Worked example · Texture size is quadratic

An 8,192 × 8,192 atlas contains about 67 million pixels. A 4,096 × 4,096 atlas contains about 16.8 million: one quarter as many. At four bytes per decoded pixel, those simple image buffers would be about 256 MiB and 64 MiB respectively, before additional working buffers. JPEG file sizes are a different, compressed quantity.

Inspect in Echo: look for blurry areas, hard exposure boundaries, distorted texture, background bleeding and inferred colour in completed regions. Switch back to the untextured mesh when you need to judge shape.

Review 09

Why might increasing atlas size fail to fix a blurred patch? Explain the different jobs of a UV seam, a gutter and seam levelling.

Suggested answer

The source photo may be blurry, badly angled or unsuitable, or the mapping may be stretched. A UV seam cuts the surface into flattenable pieces. A gutter provides filtering space around those pieces. Seam levelling adjusts colour differences where photographic sources meet. None is a substitute for correct geometric alignment.

Chapter 10 · Pipeline stage: export + run infrastructure

Portable models & reproducible stages

Distinguish the delivered model from the evidence and settings that produced it.

Explain it to a ten-year-old

Put the finished model, its coloured wrapping paper and the instructions for attaching it into a box another program can open. Keep your working notes too, so you can understand how you made it.

10.1 What GLB packages

glTF is a format for delivering 3D assets. GLB is its binary container form. Echo writes triangle positions and indices, UV coordinates and embedded photographic texture into its model export. The file describes how to display the asset; it does not by itself carry all intermediate depth maps or establish measurement accuracy.

Echo’s export stage writes cliff.glb, geographic-reference metadata and a reconstruction report. The report includes such information as camera and point counts, face count, texture coverage, inferred-colour faces, warnings and source-artifact references. Optional export validation inspects the GLB structure. It verifies a file-format concern, not whether the cliff is physically correct.

10.2 Other outputs have different jobs

The cloud and mesh stages also produce PLY artifacts. PLY can store point or mesh data; it is not interchangeable with the complete textured GLB package. Echo has a separate GIS export service for geographic data. An ordinary model export and a GIS export should not be treated as identical operations.

10.3 Checkpoints and reuse

Echo’s checkpoint store hashes the stage, declared inputs, storage version and algorithm identity into an artifact key. If a matching committed artifact exists, the store checks its manifest and file hashes before reuse. Otherwise it builds in a temporary workspace, writes metadata and commits the completed artifact. This supports resuming expensive processing and inspecting intermediate results.

Stages can have finer units, such as one feature image or one matched pair. Reusing them avoids repeating unaffected work. The dependency declarations determine whether a saved artifact is compatible; “there is a file with the right name” is not sufficient.

A practical Echo rule

Resume retains a run’s reconstruction settings. Start a new run when you want changed reconstruction settings applied. Algorithm changes also need appropriate identity changes so cached results are not silently confused with results from a different method.

10.4 CPU, Metal and memory planning

The processing application is C# split into subsystems; the macOS desktop is native SwiftUI/AppKit with SceneKit/Metal viewing. Metal accelerates selected calculations, while resource planners manage concurrency and working memory. Paged storage lets some datasets exceed in-memory budgets. These mechanisms make processing possible or faster; they do not add missing photographic evidence.

Numerical paths are not all identical. For example, depth has CPU, Metal and validated-Metal modes, and implementation identity participates in relevant artifact inputs. Compare reconstruction settings, backend metadata and source evidence before assuming two runs should be identical.

Review 10

A GLB passes validation but has a smooth bulge where the cliff was never photographed. Is that contradictory? Why should you save the report along with the model?

Suggested answer

No. Format validity concerns whether the asset is structurally readable. The bulge concerns reconstruction fidelity and may come from inferred completion. The report helps preserve warnings, reference information and links to the processing evidence that a standalone model cannot explain.

Chapter 11 · Apply what you learned

Investigate one reconstruction

Trace a visible problem backwards to the earliest stage with evidence of the problem.

Choose a saved Echo project with a recognisable rock feature: a crack, ledge or overhang. Keep that feature as your reference through all stages. Record actual observations rather than expected ones. These exercises do not require changing application code.

A six-stop inspection

  1. Source photos: identify at least three views containing the feature. Note sharpness, overlap, shadows, visible scale change and occlusion. If only one view sees it clearly, record that limitation.
  2. Features and matches: find nearby features. Check whether their links land on the same physical detail. Distinguish an attractive-looking match from a geometrically accepted match.
  3. Alignment: inspect the camera arrangement and sparse cloud. Does the arrangement make sense given where you stood? Note unplaced images and any relevant report warnings.
  4. Depth and cloud: inspect the feature’s depth in individual views. Then find it in the cloud. If it disappears between these stages, consider cross-view validation before blaming meshing.
  5. Mesh: hide the texture. Look for relief, thickness, holes, floating fragments and smooth inferred bridges. Compare these with the cloud.
  6. Texture: restore colour. Note whether detail is geometric or only photographic, and whether seams or blur align with changes in source view.

Capstone · Explain an overhang

A capture set mostly shows the cliff front. The final textured mesh appears to fill the dark underside of an overhang with a smooth coloured surface. Write a short account of how this could happen, identifying measured evidence, rejected evidence, inferred geometry and inferred appearance. List two inspections that would test your account.

One plausible explanation

The underside was poorly observed, shadowed or occluded. Stereo either found no reliable depth or its estimates failed cross-view checks. The cloud therefore had a gap. Mesh completion inferred a surface near the observed boundary, and colour completion filled parts without suitable photographic coverage. Inspect the underside in the original views and depth maps, then compare the dense cloud with the untextured completed mesh. This is a hypothesis to test; incorrect alignment could create a different failure with a similar appearance.

The vocabulary, in plain language

Artifact
A saved stage result, such as features, a depth map or a mesh, with associated metadata.
Atlas
A texture image containing multiple packed surface charts.
Back-projection
Turning a pixel plus depth into a 3D position using the camera model.
Baseline
The separation between camera centres.
Bundle adjustment
Jointly refining cameras and scene points to improve agreement with image observations.
Chart / UV island
A connected piece of surface flattened into texture coordinates.
Cheirality
The requirement that a visible reconstructed point lies in front of its observing camera.
Correspondence
Two or more observations believed to show the same physical detail.
Descriptor
A numerical description of appearance around a detected feature.
Depth map
A per-view array of depth values, with invalid entries where estimates are unavailable.
Difference of Gaussians
The difference between two differently blurred images, used to find structure at a particular scale.
Epipolar line
The line in another image on which the correspondence to a point should lie under the two-view geometry.
Essential matrix
A relation between calibrated views that encodes relative rotation and translation direction.
Extrinsics / pose
A camera’s location and orientation relative to a chosen scene frame.
Fundamental matrix
The matrix describing the epipolar relation between two image-coordinate systems.
Graph cut
A method for separating a graph into groups at low cost; Echo uses binary cuts inside source-view labelling.
Gutter
Padding around a UV chart to protect its texture from filtering across unrelated neighbours.
Inlier / outlier
An observation that agrees / disagrees with the fitted model under a chosen tolerance.
Intrinsics
Internal camera parameters, such as focal length and principal point.
Inverse depth
The quantity 1/Z; useful because it relates simply to stereo image displacement.
Normal
A direction perpendicular to a surface, indicating its orientation.
Occlusion
A nearer surface hiding something farther away from a particular camera.
Octave
A level of a scale pyramid at a different image resolution, typically separated by a factor of two.
Octree
A hierarchical 3D partition where a cell can split into eight smaller cells.
Parallax
A change in apparent position or viewing direction caused by camera translation.
Point cloud
A collection of 3D points, potentially with colours and confidence information.
Prior
Additional information that guides an estimate, such as an approximate GPS position.
Quadric error
A compact estimate of deviation from local planes, used to choose mesh collapses.
RANSAC
Repeatedly fitting models to small random samples and finding a large agreeing consensus.
Reprojection error
The pixel difference between a measured image observation and the projected 3D estimate.
Schur complement
Block elimination that reduces a coupled system before recovering the eliminated variables.
Screened Poisson
Surface reconstruction from oriented samples with additional point-position constraints.
SfM
Structure from Motion: estimating sparse structure and camera poses from overlapping images.
MVS
Multi-View Stereo: estimating dense geometry using multiple images with known or estimated camera geometry.
Texel
A pixel in a texture image.
Track
A collection of image observations associated with one scene landmark.
TSDF
A truncated signed distance function, combining surface-distance evidence within a bounded influence region.
UV
Two-dimensional coordinates mapping a mesh surface to a texture image.
Voxel
A small cell in a spatial grid; the 3D counterpart of a pixel.
Zero crossing
Where a signed field changes sign, identifying an implicit surface.
